# United Kingdom — Emergency Reference

> **This document assumes the internet is unavailable.** It summarises what the UK government,
> devolved administrations and local responders have planned and advised, as of 26 October 2026.
> If official instructions (radio, TV, Emergency Alert, emergency services) differ from this
> document, **follow the official instruction**.

**Version 0.1 — 26 October 2026.** Unofficial publication, compiled from public sources. Use **at
your own risk**; the compilers accept **no liability**. See the colophon at the end.

## A. QUICK REFERENCE (first pages — print separately)

### A.1 First 5 minutes

1. **Get inside.** Close windows and doors; if there is smoke, gas or a chemical release, shut off
   ventilation and block gaps.<sup id="ref-A-1"><a href="#note-A-1">[1]</a></sup>
2. **Tune in.** BBC local radio or BBC Radio 4 on a battery or wind-up FM radio. Write the
   frequency on the radio now.<sup id="ref-A-2"><a href="#note-A-2">[2]</a></sup>
3. **Check your phone.** A UK Emergency Alert stays on screen; read the whole message.
4. **Warn and help your neighbours** — especially anyone frail, alone, disabled or not fluent in
   English.
5. **Stay in until it is safe.** Follow the emergency services; do not go and look.

> **"Go in, stay in, tune in."** That is the official UK advice for most incidents — unless there is
> an obvious danger to the building (fire, structural damage), going inside and listening for
> instructions is usually the safest action.<sup id="ref-A-3"><a href="#note-A-3">[3]</a></sup>

### A.3 Warnings and alerting

| Signal | What it looks like | Meaning | What you do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **UK Emergency Alert** | Loud siren-like sound, vibration, full-screen text on your phone or tablet | The government or emergency services are warning you about a danger to life nearby | Read the whole message; follow its instructions; tell others |
| **Emergency Alert test** | Same sound, message says it is a test | Test only | Nothing |
| **Flood alert** (orange triangle, one wavy line) | Issued by the Environment Agency | Flooding is **possible** — prepare | Be ready to act; check your flood plan; move livestock and equipment |
| **Flood warning** (red triangle, two wavy lines) | Environment Agency | Flooding is **expected** — act now | Move people, pets and valuables to safety; go to higher ground or an upper floor; turn off gas, electricity and water if safe |
| **Severe flood warning** (red triangle, three wavy lines) | Environment Agency | Flooding **could risk life** and cause significant disruption | Stay in a safe place; be ready to evacuate; call 999 if in immediate danger |
| **Met Office warning: yellow / amber / red** | Weather forecast and app | Yellow: be aware. Amber: increased likelihood of impacts. Red: dangerous weather, risk to life — act now | Read the detail; change plans; avoid travel in red |
| **BBC local radio / Radio 4** | Radio | Official emergency information | Keep listening; this is your most reliable source when power and internet fail |
| **Door-to-door / loudhailer** | Police, fire or council | Local evacuation or instruction | Follow it immediately |

**How Emergency Alerts work.** Mobile masts in the affected area broadcast the alert; every
compatible phone or tablet in range receives it. It does **not** need mobile data or wifi, it is
**free**, and you do **not** sign up. It runs on **4G and 5G** networks. You will **not** receive it
if your device is turned off, connected to a 2G or 3G network, wifi-only, or incompatible; you *may
not* receive it in airplane mode. Compatible: **iPhone iOS 14.5 or later**, **Android 11 or later**.
Your phone number and location are **not** collected.
<sup id="ref-A-4"><a href="#note-A-4">[4]</a></sup>

## B. HOW THE UK IS ORGANISED FOR CRISES

### B.1 Overview: who does what

| Level | Body | Role |
|---|---|---|
| National (political) | **COBR** — Cabinet Office Briefing Rooms | Cross-government crisis meeting, chaired by the Prime Minister or a senior minister, convened for significant national emergencies |
| National (coordination) | **Civil Contingencies Secretariat** (Cabinet Office) | Coordinates cross-government resilience, owns the National Risk Register, supports COBR |
| National (lead) | **Lead Government Department (LGD)** | The department that leads the response for a given risk (e.g. flooding → Defra/Environment Agency; health → DHSC/UKHSA; energy → DESNZ) |
| Regional (England) | **Local Resilience Forum (LRF)** | Multi-agency planning and response body for a police area, built on the Civil Contingencies Act 2004 |
| Regional (Scotland) | **Regional Resilience Partnership (RRP)** | Scottish equivalent of the LRF |
| Regional (Wales) | **Local Resilience Forum (Wales)** | Four LRFs in Wales |
| Regional (NI) | **Emergency Preparedness Group (EPG)** | Northern Ireland equivalent |
| Local | **Local authority, police, fire, ambulance, NHS, Environment Agency** | Category 1 responders with statutory duties |
| Local (voluntary) | Red Cross, St John Ambulance, RNLI, mountain rescue, search and rescue | Category 2 responders and volunteers |

### B.2 The Civil Contingencies Act 2004

The Act has two parts with very different consequences for you.

**Part 1 — local responders' duties.** It defines an "emergency" as an event or situation that
threatens serious damage to human welfare, to the environment, or (in the case of war or terrorism)
to the security of the United Kingdom. Damage to human welfare specifically includes loss of life,
illness or injury, homelessness, damage to property, disruption of supplies of money, food, water,
energy or fuel, disruption of communication systems, disruption of transport, and disruption of
health services.<sup id="ref-B-1"><a href="#note-B-1">[1]</a></sup>

It creates two categories of responder:

- **Category 1** (core duties): local authorities, police, fire and rescue authorities, ambulance
  services, NHS bodies, the Environment Agency and (in Wales) Natural Resources Wales. They must
  assess risk, plan, maintain business continuity, warn and inform the public, and share
  information.
- **Category 2** (cooperating responders): utilities, transport operators, telephone providers,
  health and safety regulators and others. They must cooperate and share information.

Every LRF publishes a **Community Risk Register** for its area — the local version of the National
Risk Register.

**Part 2 — emergency powers.** This is the part that can change your legal rights, and it has
deliberate safeguards, often called the "triple lock":

1. An emergency has occurred, is occurring or is about to occur;
2. it is necessary to make provision to prevent, control or mitigate an aspect or effect of it;
3. the need for that provision is **urgent**.
<sup id="ref-B-3"><a href="#note-B-3">[3]</a></sup>

Emergency regulations can be made by Order in Council, or by a senior minister (the Prime Minister
or a Secretary of State) if waiting for an Order in Council would cause serious delay.
<sup id="ref-B-2"><a href="#note-B-2">[2]</a></sup>

**Hard limits on emergency regulations:**

- They may only be made so far as is **appropriate** to the emergency and **in due proportion** to
  its effects.
- They must state which parts of the UK they apply to.
- They **cannot require anyone to provide military service**.
- They **cannot prohibit participation in a strike** or other industrial action.
- They cannot create serious criminal offences: any offence must be triable only summarily, with a
  maximum of **3 months' imprisonment** or a level 5 fine.
- They **cannot amend Part 2 of the Act itself or the Human Rights Act 1998**.
<sup id="ref-B-4"><a href="#note-B-4">[4]</a></sup>

**Duration:** emergency regulations **lapse after 30 days** unless made again sooner. Lapsing does
not undo anything already done under them, and new regulations can be made.
<sup id="ref-B-5"><a href="#note-B-5">[5]</a></sup>

**What this means in practice:** the UK has no standing "state of emergency" that suspends normal
law. Emergency powers are temporary, proportionate, time-limited and cannot touch the Human Rights
Act. Parliament is recalled and the regulations are scrutinised.

### B.3 National risk planning

The **National Risk Register (NRR)** is the public version of the government's classified National
Security Risk Assessment. The current edition is **NRR 2026**, published on **12 August 2026**,
superseding NRR 2025 (16 January 2025); the NSRA now runs as a "dynamic assessment process" with
risks reassessed more often.
<sup id="ref-B-6"><a href="#note-B-6">[6]</a></sup>

For each risk the NRR sets out a **reasonable worst-case scenario** — not a prediction, but the worst
plausible manifestation once highly unlikely variations are discounted.
<sup id="ref-B-7"><a href="#note-B-7">[7]</a></sup>

Risk areas covered include terrorism (including CBRN and attacks on infrastructure), cyber attacks on
energy, health, transport, telecoms and finance, state threats, disruption to gas and oil supplies,
accidents and systems failures (including reservoir or dam collapse and loss of drinking water),
natural hazards (wildfire, volcanic eruption, earthquake, severe space weather, storms, heatwaves, low
temperatures, coastal, fluvial and surface-water flooding, drought, poor air quality), and human,
animal and plant health (pandemic, emerging infectious disease, foot and mouth disease).
<sup id="ref-B-8"><a href="#note-B-8">[8]</a></sup>

**What changed in 2026.** The NRR 2026 adds, among others:

- **digital resilience failure** — based on lessons from the CrowdStrike IT outage of July 2024;
- **cyber attack on water infrastructure** — following reported attacks on the water sector;
- **two data-infrastructure risks** (a cyber attack and a disruption) — the sector became a
  critical national infrastructure sub-sector in 2024;
- **interference in democratic processes**;
- **significant disruption to the criminal justice system**; and
- **accidental damage to the national gas transmission network**.

Scoring was also adjusted for some existing risks. The NSRA now updates risks "as frequently as
needed", and a refreshed Expert Advisory Programme is used to challenge "groupthink".
<sup id="ref-B-9"><a href="#note-B-9">[9]</a></sup>
<sup id="ref-B-10"><a href="#note-B-10">[10]</a></sup>

Announcements alongside the 2026 edition included a national public awareness campaign, a formalised
role for regional mayors in local resilience under a consultation on the Civil Contingencies Act
2004, and the largest UK home defence exercise in decades — **Exercise ALBISTON SHADOW** — planned for
2027 to test hybrid attacks.<sup id="ref-B-11"><a href="#note-B-11">[11]</a></sup>

The NRR is written for responders and businesses. Household advice sits separately on
**GOV.UK/Prepare** and the **Be Ready** campaign, and each LRF's Community Risk Register is the local
picture.<sup id="ref-B-12"><a href="#note-B-12">[12]</a></sup>

### B.4 Local resilience

- **Local Resilience Forums (LRFs)** are the multi-agency planning bodies for each police area in
  England and Wales. They bring together councils, emergency services, the NHS, the Environment
  Agency and utilities to plan for and respond to local emergencies.
- **Scotland** uses **Regional Resilience Partnerships**; **Northern Ireland** uses **Emergency
  Preparedness Groups**. These play the same convening role as LRFs.
  <sup id="ref-B-7"><a href="#note-B-7">[7]</a></sup>
- LRFs publish **Community Risk Registers** identifying the highest-priority risks in their area.
  Your LRF's register is the most relevant risk document for your street.
- In a live incident the response is coordinated through the police (who normally chair the
  multi-agency "gold" command) unless the incident is led by another agency — for example the
  Environment Agency for flooding of main rivers, or a local authority for rest centres.

### B.6 Military and civil defence

- The UK has **no conscription**. The National Service Act 1948 introduced post-war national
  service; the last national servicemen were discharged in **1963**, and the legal basis for
  peacetime conscription no longer exists. There is currently **no legal mechanism** to conscript
  people into the armed forces. [VERIFY: current legislative position]
- Military support to civil authorities is called **MACA** — Military Aid to the Civil Authorities.
  It covers **MACC** (civil authorities, e.g. flood defences), **MAGD** (government departments) and
  **MCPA** (the police, including counter-terrorism). It is provided on request, is normally
  temporary, and is paid for by the requesting authority where a statutory duty exists.
- The **Armed Forces reserves** can be called out under the Reserve Forces Act 1996 for national
  danger, warlike operations and certain other purposes.
- The UK's **nuclear deterrent** is **Trident**, based on four Vanguard-class submarines at HM Naval
  Base Clyde (Faslane), with Trident II D5 missiles and warheads from the Atomic Weapons
  Establishment. The "continuous at-sea deterrent" means at least one boat is deployed at all times.
- The UK is a founding **NATO** member. NATO Article 5 — an attack on one is an attack on all — has
  been invoked once, after the attacks of 11 September 2001.
- **Nuclear war planning:** the UK maintains a **Standing Defence Nuclear Command** arrangement so
  that constitutional authority to act survives a decapitation strike. Letters of last resort are
  written by each Prime Minister. [VERIFY: current arrangements]

### B.7 How the government will communicate with you

**In order of reliability when the internet fails:**

1. **Emergency Alert** — cell broadcast to your phone; no data, no signup, works under network load.
2. **BBC local radio and BBC Radio 4** — the designated emergency broadcasters; they stay on air
   during power outages.
3. **TV and social media** — only while power, transmission and internet survive.
4. **Door-to-door and loudhailer** — police, fire and council at street level.
5. **Rest centres and community hubs** — physical information points.

**Recognising a genuine message:**

- An Emergency Alert is a full-screen message with a siren-like sound, giving the area, the agency
  that sent it, what is happening, and what to do. It is also published on **GOV.UK/alerts**.
- The government will **never** ask for money, passwords or bank details by alert, text or email.
- Check a second source: BBC local radio or the emergency services' social media.
- Do not forward unverified messages. Misinformation spreads fastest in the first hours.

### C.1 Flooding (coastal, river, surface water, reservoir)

#### C.1.1 Risk in the UK

- Flooding is one of the UK's most significant natural hazards and appears three times in the
  National Risk Register: **coastal**, **fluvial** and **surface water** flooding.
  <sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup>
- The NRR's reasonable worst-case coastal scenario is flooding across the **east coast of England**,
  affecting a very large number of homes, with comprehensive warnings, mass evacuation and shelter,
  fatalities and casualties, and large areas of road and rail flooded.
  <sup id="ref-C-2"><a href="#note-C-2">[2]</a></sup>
- Sea level rise increases coastal risk over time. Surface-water ("flash") flooding can happen
  anywhere, not only near a river, and is **not** covered by Environment Agency flood warnings.
  <sup id="ref-C-3"><a href="#note-C-3">[3]</a></sup>

#### C.1.2 Official plan

- **England:** the **Environment Agency** issues flood warnings for rivers, the sea and groundwater
  and maintains defences on main rivers and the coast; **lead local flood authorities** (county and
  unitary councils) lead on surface water and ordinary watercourses; **internal drainage boards**
  manage low-lying land.
- **Wales:** **Natural Resources Wales**. **Scotland:** **SEPA**. **Northern Ireland:** **DAERA**.
- **Evacuation** is ordered by the police or the local authority, normally through multi-agency
  "gold" command. Rest centres and emergency accommodation are arranged by the local council.
- **Warning lead times:** a flood alert is normally issued **2 to 12 hours** before flooding; a flood
  warning **30 minutes to 2 hours** before.<sup id="ref-C-3"><a href="#note-C-3">[3]</a></sup>

#### C.1.3 Warning

| Level | Meaning | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **Flood alert** (orange triangle, one wavy line) | Flooding is **possible** — prepare | Check your flood plan; have insurance documents and medication ready; move livestock and equipment; avoid flood water |
| **Flood warning** (red triangle, two wavy lines) | Flooding is **expected** — act now | Move people, pets and valuables to safety; go to higher ground or an upper floor; turn off gas, electricity and water if safe; deploy flood protection; follow the emergency services |
| **Severe flood warning** (red triangle, three wavy lines) | Flooding **could risk life** and cause significant community disruption | Stay in a safe place; be ready to evacuate; call **999** if in immediate danger; if caught in a flash flood, get to higher ground or an upper floor |

<sup id="ref-C-3"><a href="#note-C-3">[3]</a></sup>

**Sign up:** free flood warnings by phone, text or email for England (rivers, sea and groundwater —
**not** surface water). By phone through **Floodline 0345 988 1188** (textphone 0345 602 6340),
24 hours.<sup id="ref-C-4"><a href="#note-C-4">[4]</a></sup>

**Early signs:** water rising fast in a river or stream; the sound of rushing water; water coming out
of drains; cracks, seepage or collapse at the foot of an embankment; sewage backing up.

#### C.1.4 Before

- Check your long-term flood risk (rivers, sea, surface water, reservoirs, groundwater) and the
  5-day flood forecast.<sup id="ref-C-3"><a href="#note-C-3">[3]</a></sup>
- **Make a flood plan** — personal, community/group or business.
  <sup id="ref-C-5"><a href="#note-C-5">[5]</a></sup>
- Know how to turn off your gas, electricity and water.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>
- Move valuables, electrical items and documents upstairs or into waterproof containers.
- Get flood protection: floodgates, air-brick covers, non-return valves, sandbags.
- Check your insurance covers flood (see G.4 — **Flood Re** matters here).
- Keep a **grab bag** by the door.
- Identify your nearest **community hub**, rest centre and higher ground.
- Find local support: **flood action groups**, **community flood wardens** and **community hubs**
  offering food, clothing, shelter and advice during a flood.
  <sup id="ref-C-5"><a href="#note-C-5">[5]</a></sup>

#### C.1.5 During

**If you are told to evacuate:**
1. Leave immediately. Delaying or refusing to evacuate may put your life, and responders' lives, at
   risk.<sup id="ref-C-7"><a href="#note-C-7">[7]</a></sup>
2. Turn off gas, electricity and water if it is safe.
3. Take your grab bag, medication, documents, phone and charger.
4. Do not walk, cycle or drive through flood water.
5. Follow the route the emergency services give you.
6. Tell a neighbour or leave a note saying where you have gone.

**If you cannot leave:**
1. Go to the highest floor; take water, food, medication, a torch and a radio.
2. Do not go into a cellar or basement.
3. Call **999** if your life is in danger.
4. Signal from a window so rescuers can see you.
5. If caught by a flash flood outdoors, move to higher ground immediately.

**Never drive through flood water** — it is often deeper and faster than it looks, and manhole covers
can be missing.

#### C.1.6 After

- Do not return home until the authorities say it is safe.
- Assume flood water is contaminated with sewage: wear gloves and boots, wash hands, cover cuts.
- Photograph all damage **before** moving anything, for insurance.
- Throw away food that has touched flood water; use bottled water until told otherwise.
- Gas and electricity must be checked by a qualified engineer before reconnection.
- Contact your council about emergency accommodation if your home is uninhabitable.
  <sup id="ref-C-8"><a href="#note-C-8">[8]</a></sup>
- Beware of damp, mould, electrical hazards and hidden structural damage.

#### C.1.7 Where to go

- **Rest centres** and emergency accommodation — arranged by your local council.
- **Community hubs** — food, clothing, shelter and advice during a flood.
  <sup id="ref-C-5"><a href="#note-C-5">[5]</a></sup>
- Higher ground, or an upper floor of a sturdy building.
- Family or friends outside the flood area if you can travel safely.

#### C.1.8 Checklist

- [ ] Long-term flood risk checked for my address
- [ ] Signed up for free flood warnings (Floodline 0345 988 1188)
- [ ] Flood plan written and discussed
- [ ] Know how to turn off gas, electricity and water
- [ ] Flood protection in place or planned
- [ ] Insurance checked, including flood cover
- [ ] Grab bag packed and by the door
- [ ] Valuables and documents moved upstairs
- [ ] Nearest community hub and rest centre identified
- [ ] Neighbours who need help identified

### C.2 Severe weather (storms, heat, cold, snow, drought)

#### C.2.1 Risk in the UK

Storms, heatwaves, low temperatures and snow, drought and poor air quality each appear as separate
risks in the National Risk Register 2026.<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup> The Be Ready
campaign was launched partly because "storms last winter and the wildfires this summer" showed how
severe weather disrupts power, water, phones and the internet.
<sup id="ref-C-9"><a href="#note-C-9">[9]</a></sup>

#### C.2.2 Official plan

The **Met Office** issues UK weather warnings in three colours:

| Colour | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Yellow** | Be aware. Issued for a range of situations — read the detail |
| **Amber** | Increased likelihood of impacts: travel delays, road and rail closures, power cuts, risk to life and property |
| **Red** | Dangerous weather expected — take action now. Very likely a risk to life, with substantial disruption to travel and energy supplies, and possibly widespread damage |

<sup id="ref-C-10"><a href="#note-C-10">[10]</a></sup>

Warnings are judged on both **likelihood** (e.g. "high, 50–75%") and **impact**, and the Met Office
advises being prepared for warnings to change quickly.
<sup id="ref-C-10"><a href="#note-C-10">[10]</a></sup>

#### C.2.3 Warning

- Met Office app, website, and broadcast media.
- Red warnings are widely broadcast and may be accompanied by Emergency Alerts for local impacts.
- For power cuts: your network operator's cut map and **105**.

#### C.2.4 Before

- Sign up for alerts and warnings; learn the hazards in your area.
- Keep torches, batteries, a power bank and warm clothing ready — "people cope better with power cuts
  when they have prepared for them in advance".
  <sup id="ref-C-10"><a href="#note-C-10">[10]</a></sup>
- Secure loose outdoor items before a storm; park away from trees.
- **Heat:** close curtains on sunny sides, keep windows shut during the day when it is hotter outside,
  drink regularly, check on older and vulnerable neighbours.
- **Cold:** know where your stopcock is, lag exposed pipes, keep one room warm.
- **Winter car kit:** torch, in-car charger, warm clothes and blankets, high-visibility clothing,
  jump leads, food and drink, shovel; a first aid kit is also sensible.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>

#### C.2.5 During

- **Red warning:** do not travel unless essential; stay indoors.
- Stay away from the coast during storms — large waves can sweep people off their feet and out to
  sea. Keep dogs on a lead near cliffs.
  <sup id="ref-C-10"><a href="#note-C-10">[10]</a></sup>
- Report power cuts on **105** (England, Scotland, Wales) or **03457 643643** (Northern Ireland).
- Before power returns, switch off and unplug irons, ovens, heaters, TVs and computers.
- Keep fridge and freezer doors closed.
- Use torches, not candles.
- In heat: avoid strenuous activity in the hottest part of the day; never leave anyone in a parked
  car; check on people who live alone.

#### C.2.6 After

- Check on neighbours, especially anyone older, ill or living alone.
- Photograph damage before repairs, for insurance.
- Check food in the fridge and freezer; discard anything that has warmed up.
- Have a qualified person check electrical equipment affected by a power cut or water.

#### C.2.7 Where to go

- Stay at home unless told otherwise.
- Libraries, shopping centres and community centres are often designated cool spaces in heatwaves;
  your council will say which. [VERIFY per council]

#### C.2.8 Checklist

- [ ] Met Office app installed with warnings enabled
- [ ] Torch, batteries and power bank ready
- [ ] Warm clothing and blankets
- [ ] Winter car kit packed
- [ ] Power cut number known (105 / 03457 643643)
- [ ] Vulnerable neighbours identified

### C.3 Wildfire

#### C.3.1 Risk in the UK

Wildfire is a named risk in the National Risk Register, alongside volcanic eruption, earthquake and
severe space weather.<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup> The Be Ready campaign cites
"the wildfires this summer" as one of the events showing how severe weather disrupts services.
<sup id="ref-C-9"><a href="#note-C-9">[9]</a></sup>

#### C.3.2 Official plan

Fire and rescue services lead the response, supported by landowners (Forestry England, Forestry and
Land Scotland, Natural Resources Wales, National Trust), the police for evacuation and road closures,
and local authorities for rest centres. Fire-danger ratings are issued by the Met Office and the fire
services. [VERIFY: current fire severity index source]

#### C.3.3 Warning

- Fire severity index and local fire service advice.
- Emergency Alert or police loudhailer for evacuation.
- Early signs: smell of smoke, haze or ash, an orange glow on the horizon, aircraft and helicopters,
  road closures.

#### C.3.4 Before

- Do not light fires, barbecues or campfires in or near dry vegetation; follow any total fire ban.
- Never discard cigarettes or glass in the countryside.
- Keep access tracks clear so fire engines can get through; do not park in gateways.
- Know two ways out of your area.
- Have a grab bag ready.

#### C.3.5 During

1. **Call 999** and give the exact location.
2. Leave early — do not wait to be told if you can see fire approaching.
3. Move **away** from the fire and across its path, not in front of it.
4. Close windows and air vents in the car; do not stop in smoke.
5. If trapped, go to a large open area with little vegetation, or into a building, and call 999.
6. Follow the emergency services' instructions exactly.

#### C.3.6 After

- Stay off burnt ground: roots can smoulder and trees can fall.
- Do not return until the fire service says it is safe.
- Photograph damage for insurance.

#### C.3.7 Where to go

- Follow the evacuation route given by police or fire service.
- Rest centres are arranged by the local authority.

#### C.3.8 Checklist

- [ ] Two escape routes from my area known
- [ ] Grab bag ready
- [ ] Local fire service advice checked in dry weather
- [ ] Nothing burnable left outside in dry spells

### C.4 Power cut and prolonged blackout

#### C.4.1 Risk in the UK

Cyber attacks on electricity infrastructure and other failures are named risks in the National Risk
Register.<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup> Most outages are short, but a national
energy shortage could trigger **rota disconnection** — planned, rotating cuts.

#### C.4.2 Official plan

- **Distribution network operators** run the local grid and restore supply; the **National Energy
  System Operator (NESO)** balances the national system.
- In a national shortfall, **rota disconnection** would cut areas in turn. Your area is identified by
  a **"load block letter"**, and each rotation would last roughly **3 hours**.
  <sup id="ref-C-11"><a href="#note-C-11">[11]</a></sup>
- **Priority Services Register (PSR)** customers — people with a health condition or reduced
  mobility, pensionable age, medical dependence on supply, children under 5, communication needs, a
  mental health or cognitive condition, or a temporary need after hospital or bereavement — get
  advance warning and extra support. In Northern Ireland these are **Customer Care Registers**.
  <sup id="ref-C-12"><a href="#note-C-12">[12]</a></sup>
- Telecare and landline providers may supply a battery backup unit for a minimum of **one hour**.
  <sup id="ref-C-11"><a href="#note-C-11">[11]</a></sup>

#### C.4.3 Warning

- Report and check outages on **105** (England, Scotland, Wales) or **03457 643643** (Northern
  Ireland); operator websites show live maps.
- PSR customers get advance notice.
- Early signs of rota disconnection: public warnings of a capacity shortfall, appeals to cut demand,
  advance notice to PSR customers.

#### C.4.4 Before

- Find your **load block letter** at powercut105.com and write it down.
- Torch, batteries, power bank, battery or wind-up radio.
- Cash — card machines and ATMs fail.
- Keep a charged mobile; emergency calls roam onto any available network.
- Fill a thermos with hot water; keep blankets together.
- Know how to open garage doors and gates without power.
- Register for the PSR if eligible.

#### C.4.5 During

1. Switch off and unplug irons, ovens, heaters, TVs and computers so they do not surge when power
   returns.
2. Keep fridge and freezer closed.
3. Use torches, not candles.
4. Keep one room warm and live in it.
5. Check on neighbours.
6. Call **105**, not 999, unless there is a genuine emergency.
7. If you depend on medical equipment, contact your supplier and your care provider.

#### C.4.6 After

- Check food that has warmed up and throw it away.
- Check smoke alarms, heating and medical equipment before relying on them.
- Reset clocks and timers.
- Report damage to your network operator.

#### C.4.7 Where to go

- Stay at home unless told otherwise.
- Community hubs and council buildings may have power and information.
- PSR customers can ask their operator for extra support.

#### C.4.8 Checklist

- [ ] Load block letter known
- [ ] 105 / 03457 643643 written down
- [ ] Torches, batteries, power bank, radio
- [ ] Cash at home
- [ ] PSR registration checked
- [ ] Medical equipment plan agreed with supplier

### C.5 Telecommunications failure

#### C.5.1 Risk in the UK

Cyber attacks on **telecommunications systems** and on digital infrastructure are named risks, as is
**total loss of transatlantic telecommunications cables**.
<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup> Emergency Alerts depend on 4G/5G masts, so a
telecoms failure also removes a warning channel.

#### C.5.2 Official plan

Ofcom regulates telecoms; operators must maintain resilience. Government guidance tells households to
keep a battery or wind-up radio precisely because alerts and internet may fail together.
<sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>

#### C.5.3 Warning

- Loss of mobile signal, broadband, or both; failures of card payments and apps.
- A **landline** may still work if only mobile is down, and vice versa.

#### C.5.4 Before

- Write down important phone numbers on paper.
- Keep a battery or wind-up radio.
- Keep cash.
- Know your provider's fault number.
- Agree a physical meeting point with family, since calls may fail.

#### C.5.5 During

- Use text messages — they often get through when calls fail.
- Try a landline, or a neighbour's connection.
- Listen to the radio for information.
- Go to a community hub or library for internet access.
- Do not assume an online rumour is true; check the radio.

#### C.5.6 After

- Check bank and card statements for fraud after an outage.
- Change passwords if you suspect compromise.
- Report faults to your provider and keep a record for compensation claims.

#### C.5.7 Where to go

- Libraries, community hubs and council buildings for internet and information.

#### C.5.8 Checklist

- [ ] Paper list of phone numbers
- [ ] Battery or wind-up radio
- [ ] Cash
- [ ] Meeting point agreed
- [ ] Provider fault number written down

### C.6 Drinking water loss and sewer flooding

#### C.6.1 Risk in the UK

**Water infrastructure failure or loss of drinking water**, and **reservoir/dam collapse**, are named
risks in the National Risk Register 2026.<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup> Sewer
flooding commonly follows heavy rain.

#### C.6.2 Official plan

Water companies must maintain supply and hold emergency plans; they distribute bottled water when
supply fails. Local authorities lead on rest centres and public health advice during an outage.
[VERIFY: statutory duty and timescales]

#### C.6.3 Warning

- **"Do not drink"** or boil-water notices, or loss of pressure.
- Tankers and bottled-water points announced by the water company and council.
- Sewer flooding: water backing up in toilets and drains during heavy rain.

#### C.6.4 Before

- Store **3 litres per person per day for 3 days** for drinking, and more for cooking and washing.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>
- Keep bottled water, or clean containers you can fill.
- Know where your **stopcock** is.
- Check your home's risk of surface water and sewer flooding.

#### C.6.5 During

- Follow boil or do-not-drink notices exactly; boiling does not remove every contaminant.
- Use bottled water for drinking, brushing teeth, preparing food and for babies.
- Do not drink from private wells or streams.
- For sewer flooding: keep children and pets away; wear gloves and boots; wash hands; do not use
  electrical equipment that has been wet.

#### C.6.6 After

- Flush and clean pipes as your water company advises.
- Have flooded electrics checked.
- Report sewer flooding to your water company and council.

#### C.6.7 Where to go

- Bottled-water distribution points announced by your water company.
- Community hubs and rest centres.

#### C.6.8 Checklist

- [ ] 3 days of water stored (3 litres/person/day)
- [ ] Stopcock location known
- [ ] Water company emergency number saved
- [ ] Containers for collecting water

### C.7 Food and fuel supply disruption

#### C.7.1 Risk in the UK

Disruption to **global oil trade routes** and **disruption of Russian gas supplies to Europe** are
named risks, as is **insolvency affecting fuel supply**.
<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup>

#### C.7.2 Official plan

The **Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ)** leads on fuel; the **National Energy
System Operator** balances gas and electricity. **Food supply** is led by Defra with the food industry.
Emergency powers under the Civil Contingencies Act allow rationing and requisition, within the limits
in B.2.

#### C.7.3 Warning

- Empty shelves, purchase limits, forecourt closures.
- Public appeals to reduce demand.
- Rationing would be announced officially.

#### C.7.4 Before

- Keep **3 days of non-perishable food** that needs no cooking or refrigeration: tinned meat, fish or
  vegetables, dried fruit and nuts. Buy food you would normally eat.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>
- Keep a manual tin opener.
- Do not hoard — hoarding creates the shortage.

#### C.7.5 During

- Ration what you have; plan meals.
- Do not panic-buy.
- Share with neighbours who cannot get out.
- Follow any official rationing or priority scheme.

#### C.7.6 After

- Restock; check expiry dates.
- Check food that may have spoiled during a power cut.

#### C.7.7 Where to go

- Community hubs and food banks may operate.
- Your council will announce any emergency food distribution.

#### C.7.8 Checklist

- [ ] 3 days of non-perishable food
- [ ] Tin opener
- [ ] Neighbours who may need food identified
- [ ] No hoarding

### C.8 Pandemic and infectious disease

#### C.8.1 Risk in the UK

Pandemic is a named risk in the National Risk Register, with an outbreak of an emerging infectious
disease and major animal disease outbreaks such as foot and mouth.
<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup>

COVID-19 killed **73,766** people in England and Wales in 2020 (12.1% of all deaths) and **67,350** in
2021 (11.5%), counting deaths registered as *due to* COVID-19. The ONS put the UK-wide total at
**more than 150,000** by March 2023.
<sup id="ref-C-13"><a href="#note-C-13">[13]</a></sup>
<sup id="ref-C-14"><a href="#note-C-14">[14]</a></sup>
<sup id="ref-C-15"><a href="#note-C-15">[15]</a></sup>

#### C.8.2 Official plan

- **England:** the **UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA)**, an executive agency of DHSC, covers
  infectious disease, chemicals, radiation and climate and air-quality hazards. Health protection is
  largely devolved.<sup id="ref-C-16"><a href="#note-C-16">[16]</a></sup>
- **Scotland:** **Public Health Scotland**, an NHS Board, operating since 1 April 2020.
  <sup id="ref-C-17"><a href="#note-C-17">[17]</a></sup>
- **Wales:** **Public Health Wales**, one of the 11 organisations making up NHS Wales and a Category 1
  responder under the Civil Contingencies Act.
  <sup id="ref-C-18"><a href="#note-C-18">[18]</a></sup>
- **Northern Ireland:** the **Public Health Agency (PHA)**.
  <sup id="ref-C-19"><a href="#note-C-19">[19]</a></sup>
- **Vaccination policy** is advised by the **JCVI**, an expert committee meeting three times a year.
  <sup id="ref-C-20"><a href="#note-C-20">[20]</a></sup>
- A **Pandemic Preparedness Strategy** was published on **25 March 2026**, replacing the 2011
  influenza strategy; findings from Exercise Pegasus are due in winter 2026.
  <sup id="ref-C-21"><a href="#note-C-21">[21]</a></sup>

**Stockpiles.** The UK holds **UK-wide CBRN stockpiles** and **UK-wide pandemic stockpiles** of
antibiotics, antivirals, vaccines, fluids and needles/syringes, plus **England-only pandemic PPE**.
Hospital medicines suppliers must hold at least **8 weeks'** stock and NHS Supply Chain around
**4 weeks**. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland hold their own PPE stocks.
<sup id="ref-C-22"><a href="#note-C-22">[22]</a></sup>

**Inquiry.** The UK COVID-19 Inquiry has finished public hearings; the sixth report (Care Sector) was
published **20 October 2026**, the seventh (Test, Trace, Isolate) is due **19 November 2026**, and "a
further four reports will follow".<sup id="ref-C-23"><a href="#note-C-23">[23]</a></sup>

#### C.8.3 Warning

- UKHSA and devolved public health announcements; NHS messages.
- Early signs: rising case numbers, school or workplace outbreaks, changes in testing or vaccination
  advice.

#### C.8.4 Before

- Keep vaccinations up to date; check your records.
- Keep 3 days of supplies in case you cannot go out.
- Keep paracetamol, a thermometer, soap and hand sanitiser.
- Keep a written list of your medication and doses.
- Request repeat prescriptions up to **5 working days** before you need them.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>
- Have ready-to-feed baby formula if relevant — you may not be able to boil water safely.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>

#### C.8.5 During

- Follow official guidance on distancing, isolation, masks and travel.
- Wash hands often with soap and water; cover coughs and sneezes.
- Ventilate rooms.
- Stay home if you are ill and avoid contact with people at high risk.
- Do not visit hospitals or care homes if you have symptoms.
- Look after your mental health (see F.8).

#### C.8.6 After

- Catch up on missed vaccinations and routine care.
- Watch for long-term symptoms and seek help.
- Expect the inquiry's remaining reports to change policy.

#### C.8.7 Where to go

- **111** for advice; **999** for emergencies.
- Vaccination and testing sites announced by the NHS and your council.
- Community pharmacies for advice and medicines.

#### C.8.8 Checklist

- [ ] Vaccinations up to date
- [ ] 3 days of supplies
- [ ] Medicines list written down
- [ ] Repeat prescription timing known (5 working days)
- [ ] Soap, sanitiser, thermometer, paracetamol

### C.9 Chemical incidents and industrial accidents

#### C.9.1 Risk in the UK

**COMAH** (Control of Major Accident Hazards) sites are regulated under the COMAH Regulations 2015,
which implement the Seveso III directive. Sites are **upper tier** or **lower tier**. The competent
authority is the **Health and Safety Executive (HSE)** together with the Environment Agency in
England, Natural Resources Wales in Wales, SEPA in Scotland, and HSENI with the NI Environment Agency
in Northern Ireland.<sup id="ref-C-24"><a href="#note-C-24">[24]</a></sup>

The public register of COMAH sites is searchable by area — check your neighbourhood now.
<sup id="ref-C-25"><a href="#note-C-25">[25]</a></sup>

Northern Ireland publishes a list of **28 establishments** (11 upper tier, 17 lower tier).
<sup id="ref-C-26"><a href="#note-C-26">[26]</a></sup>

Historic example: **Buncefield** oil depot, Hemel Hempstead, **11 December 2005** — explosions and a
fire that burned for several days, over 40 injured, no fatalities, large-scale evacuation, and almost
**£10 million** in fines and costs.
<sup id="ref-C-27"><a href="#note-C-27">[27]</a></sup>

**Major Accident Hazard pipelines** are regulated by HSE under the Pipelines Safety Regulations 1996.
<sup id="ref-C-28"><a href="#note-C-28">[28]</a></sup>

#### C.9.2 Official plan

- Upper-tier operators must produce a safety report and an **off-site emergency plan** with the local
  authority.
- **CHEMET** — the Met Office's chemical meteorology service — gives fire and police services plume
  and dispersion forecasts, normally emailed within **20 minutes**.
  <sup id="ref-C-29"><a href="#note-C-29">[29]</a></sup>
- The fire and rescue service leads, supported by the Environment Agency and public health bodies.

#### C.9.3 Warning

- **Emergency Alert** with "go in, stay in, tune in".
- Sirens at some major sites [VERIFY locally].
- Sight or smell: a visible plume, unusual odour, stinging eyes, coughing, dead wildlife.

#### C.9.4 Before

- Find out whether you live near a COMAH site or a major hazard pipeline; the register is searchable
  by area.<sup id="ref-C-25"><a href="#note-C-25">[25]</a></sup>
- Keep tape and plastic sheeting to seal windows, vents and doors if advised.
- Know how to turn off ventilation and air conditioning.

#### C.9.5 During

Official UK advice for a chemical release is **"go in, stay in, tune in"**:

1. **Get into a building** — any building other than the one involved. Vehicles and tents give
   inadequate protection.
2. **Close external doors and windows.**
3. **Shut off fans and air conditioning**; close air vents, including trickle vents.
4. **Do not collect children from school** — they will be sheltered there.
5. **Do not evacuate unless told to.**
6. **Tune in** to local radio or TV.
7. If exposed: **remove, remove, remove** — leave the area, remove outer clothing (this removes about
   **90%** of contamination), dry-absorb then rinse.
   <sup id="ref-C-30"><a href="#note-C-30">[30]</a></sup>

#### C.9.6 After

- Do not eat produce from an affected garden until told it is safe.
- Follow decontamination advice; bag contaminated clothing.
- Seek medical help for breathing difficulty, eye irritation or skin burns.

#### C.9.7 Where to go

- **Stay inside** — that is the default. Evacuate only on official instruction.
- If evacuated, you may be checked and decontaminated before going to a rest centre.
  <sup id="ref-C-30"><a href="#note-C-30">[30]</a></sup>

#### C.9.8 Checklist

- [ ] COMAH register checked for my area
- [ ] Ventilation shut-off known
- [ ] Tape and plastic sheeting
- [ ] Battery radio
- [ ] Know not to collect children from school

### C.10 Radiation emergency

#### C.10.1 Risk in the UK

The **Office for Nuclear Regulation (ONR)** regulates nuclear safety and security. Great Britain has
had **no nuclear accident with off-site consequences since Windscale in 1957**.
<sup id="ref-C-31"><a href="#note-C-31">[31]</a></sup>

Regulated sites include Sellafield, Dounreay, Hinkley Point A/B/C, Sizewell A/B/C, Hartlepool, Heysham
1 and 2, Torness, Dungeness A/B, Hunterston A/B, Wylfa, Oldbury, Berkeley, Bradwell, Trawsfynydd,
Chapelcross, Springfields, Capenhurst, the Low Level Waste Repository, Harwell, Winfrith, Amersham,
Lillyhall, Rosyth, Barrow, Rolls-Royce sites, AWE Aldermaston and Burghfield, and Devonport Royal
Dockyard, plus MOD sites including Vulcan, Loch Ewe, Loch Goil, Clyde Naval Base and Portsmouth.
<sup id="ref-C-32"><a href="#note-C-32">[32]</a></sup>

**REPPIR 2019** requires operators to assess hazards and requires the local authority to define a
**Detailed Emergency Planning Zone** and an **Outline Planning Zone**, prepare an off-site emergency
plan, and give prior information to the public.
<sup id="ref-C-33"><a href="#note-C-33">[33]</a></sup>

#### C.10.2 Official plan

- The local authority prepares the off-site plan for each site; the operator plans on-site.
- **Stable iodine** is distributed where there could be a risk of radioactive iodine release; all
  nuclear reactor sites with that risk have specific distribution plans.
  <sup id="ref-C-34"><a href="#note-C-34">[34]</a></sup>
- Public advice is **go in, stay in, tune in**; you may need to stay indoors for **1 to 2 days**.

#### C.10.3 Warning

- Emergency Alert, radio, TV.
- Sirens at some sites [VERIFY].
- No reliable sensory warning — radiation cannot be seen, smelled or tasted. Rely only on official
  messages.

#### C.10.4 Before

- Find out whether you live inside a Detailed Emergency Planning Zone — your council publishes this.
- Check whether you are eligible for stable iodine tablets and where they are kept.
- Know how to turn off ventilation and close windows.
- Keep a battery radio.

#### C.10.5 During

1. **Go in, stay in, tune in.** Shelter indoors.
2. Close windows and doors; turn off ventilation, fans and air conditioning.
3. **Take stable iodine only if officially told to.** It is most effective from **24 hours before** to
   **2 hours after** exposure, is still useful up to **8 hours** after, and **must not be first taken
   more than 24 hours after** exposure.
   <sup id="ref-C-34"><a href="#note-C-34">[34]</a></sup>
4. Do not go outside to collect children — schools shelter pupils.
5. Do not eat or drink anything from your garden or local sources until told it is safe.

**Why sheltering works:** sheltering in a typical house cuts inhaled exposure by about **40%** and
direct exposure by about **85%**.<sup id="ref-C-34"><a href="#note-C-34">[34]</a></sup>

#### C.10.6 After

- Leave shelter only when officially told.
- Follow advice about food, water and produce.
- Return iodine tablets or follow instructions on disposal.
- Register for health monitoring if offered.

#### C.10.7 Where to go

- **Stay indoors.** Evacuate only if instructed.
- Rest centres if evacuated; expect monitoring and decontamination first.

#### C.10.8 Checklist

- [ ] Checked whether I live in a REPPIR emergency planning zone
- [ ] Stable iodine eligibility and location known
- [ ] Ventilation shut-off known
- [ ] Battery radio
- [ ] Know: go in, stay in, tune in

### C.11 Cyber attack on critical infrastructure

#### C.11.1 Risk in the UK

The National Risk Register lists cyber attacks on **gas**, **electricity**, **civil nuclear**, **fuel
supply**, **health and social care**, **transport**, **telecommunications** and **UK retail banking**
infrastructure, plus cyber attacks on infrastructure generally and **total loss of transatlantic
telecommunications cables**.<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup>

#### C.11.2 Official plan

- The **National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC)**, part of GCHQ, is the technical authority; it issues
  advisories and supports victims.
- **Ofcom** and sector regulators oversee resilience; the **NIS Regulations** impose security and
  incident-reporting duties on essential services.
- The Civil Contingencies Act framework applies if an attack causes an "emergency" as defined in B.2.

#### C.11.3 Warning

- Services failing simultaneously across sectors; ransomware demands; official advisories.
- Emergency Alert if there is an immediate danger to life.

#### C.11.4 Before

- Install updates; use a password manager; turn on two-factor authentication.
- Keep **offline backups**.
- Keep cash.
- Keep paper copies of important numbers and documents.
- Know how to report fraud.

#### C.11.5 During

- Do not pay ransoms.
- Disconnect affected devices from the network.
- Follow NCSC and provider advice; check a second channel before believing a message.
- Do not click links in unexpected messages.
- Use cash if card payments fail.

#### C.11.6 After

- Change passwords from a clean device.
- Check bank statements.
- Report to **Action Fraud** and the NCSC.
- Restore from backup.

#### C.11.7 Where to go

- Community hubs and libraries for internet access.
- Bank branches for cash and advice.

#### C.11.8 Checklist

- [ ] Updates and 2FA on
- [ ] Offline backup
- [ ] Cash at home
- [ ] Paper copies of key information

### C.12 Terrorism and public order incidents

#### C.12.1 Risk in the UK

Terrorism is a named risk, covering international terrorist attacks, Northern Ireland-related
terrorism, attacks with explosive devices and marauding attacks in public places, attacks on
transport, CBRN attacks, conventional attacks on infrastructure and cyber attacks on infrastructure.
<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup>

#### C.12.2 Official plan

- **MI5** and **Counter Terrorism Policing** lead intelligence and investigations; the **Joint
  Terrorism Analysis Centre (JTAC)** sets the national threat level.
- Police lead the response; fire and ambulance support; local authorities open rest centres and
  family assistance centres.
- The **Terrorism Act 2000** and the **Counter-Terrorism and Border Security Act 2019** provide
  powers.

#### C.12.3 Warning

- Threat level announcements; police appeals.
- Emergency Alert if there is an ongoing danger to life.
- No warning before an attack — that is the nature of it.

#### C.12.4 Before

- Know the exits wherever you go.
- Report suspicious behaviour to the police (999 if urgent, otherwise 101, or the anti-terrorist
  hotline).
- Learn **Run, Hide, Tell**.

#### C.12.5 During

**Run, Hide, Tell** (official UK advice):
1. **Run** to a place of safety if you can. Leave your belongings.
2. **Hide** if you cannot run: lock or barricade yourself in, silence your phone, stay quiet and out
   of sight.
3. **Tell** the police by calling 999 once it is safe.
4. Follow police instructions; keep your hands visible.

**If a crowd crushes:** keep your arms in front of your chest, move with the flow, stay on your feet,
and do not bend down to pick things up.

#### C.12.6 After

- Expect the area to be cordoned off for a long time.
- Use official sources; do not spread images of victims.
- Seek support: the police set up a casualty bureau and family liaison; Samaritans is on **116 123**.
- Terrorism insurance for commercial property runs through **Pool Re** (see G.4).

#### C.12.7 Where to go

- Follow evacuation directions; move away from the incident.
- Rest centres and family assistance centres announced by police and council.

#### C.12.8 Checklist

- [ ] Exits known in places I visit
- [ ] Run, Hide, Tell understood
- [ ] Phone charged
- [ ] Meeting point agreed with household

### C.13 Armed conflict and national emergency

#### C.13.1 Risk in the UK

Armed conflict is covered by the Civil Contingencies Act definition of an emergency — war or terrorism
threatening serious damage to the security of the UK — and by Part 2 emergency powers.
<sup id="ref-C-35"><a href="#note-C-35">[35]</a></sup>

#### C.13.2 Official plan

- **COBR** coordinates; the **Armed Forces** defend the UK and NATO territory.
- **MACA** — Military Aid to the Civil Authorities — provides military support to civil responders.
- Emergency regulations under Part 2 can be made, subject to the strict limits in B.2: they **cannot**
  compel military service, **cannot** ban strikes, **cannot** create serious offences, and **cannot**
  amend the Human Rights Act.
- **There is currently no conscription** in the UK. [VERIFY current legislative position]
- In a nuclear attack the UK maintains **Standing Defence Nuclear Command** so lawful authority
  survives; each Prime Minister writes letters of last resort to submarine commanders. [VERIFY]

#### C.13.3 Warning

- Emergency Alert with instructions; sirens; broadcast alerts.
- In a nuclear attack there is **no general public attack warning today** — the **four-minute warning**
  system was decommissioned in 1992. [VERIFY]

#### C.13.4 Before

- Keep at least **3 days** of supplies; consider **7 days** if you can.
- Have a battery radio and spare batteries.
- Keep paper copies of documents.
- Identify your shelter — an internal room, basement or underground car park, away from windows.
- Agree a household plan and meeting points.

#### C.13.5 During

1. **Go in, stay in, tune in.**
2. Shelter in an internal room, basement or underground space, away from windows.
3. If outside, lie flat and protect your head; do not look at a flash.
4. Turn off gas, electricity and water if safe.
5. Listen to the radio.
6. Stay sheltered until officially told it is safe — fallout decays over days.

#### C.13.6 After

- Do not go outside until told; radioactive fallout loses intensity over time.
- Follow instructions on food, water and evacuation.
- Register with the authorities for help and to inform relatives.

#### C.13.7 Where to go

- Shelter in place; evacuate only on official instruction.

#### C.13.8 Checklist

- [ ] 3–7 days of supplies
- [ ] Battery radio
- [ ] Paper documents
- [ ] Sheltering place identified
- [ ] Household plan agreed

### C.14 Transport accidents (rail, air, sea, tunnel)

#### C.14.1 Risk in the UK

The National Risk Register lists **rail accidents**, **large passenger vessel accidents** and other
transport risks, plus terrorist attacks on transport.
<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup>

#### C.14.2 Official plan

- The **Rail Accident Investigation Branch (RAIB)** investigates rail accidents; the **Air Accidents
  Investigation Branch (AAIB)** and the **Marine Accident Investigation Branch (MAIB)** cover air and
  sea.
- The emergency services lead, with the transport operator and local authority.

#### C.14.3 Warning

- No warning. An Emergency Alert only if the surrounding area is at risk.

#### C.14.4 Before

- Know the exits in trains, aircraft and ferries.
- Listen to the safety briefing; count rows to the nearest exit.
- Keep your seatbelt on in aircraft during take-off, landing and turbulence.

#### C.14.5 During

**Train:** pull the emergency alarm; follow crew instructions; leave by the designated door; avoid
touching the electrified third rail or overhead lines.
**Aircraft:** adopt the brace position; leave belongings behind; stay low if there is smoke; count rows
to the exit.
**Ferry or ship:** go to the muster station; put on a lifejacket; follow crew instructions.
**Tunnel:** use the nearest emergency exit; stay low under smoke.

#### C.14.6 After

- Move well away from the wreckage and any fire.
- Report to the emergency services so you can be accounted for.
- Seek medical attention even if you feel fine — shock, smoke inhalation and internal injury can be
  delayed.
- Expect investigation cordons to remain for a long time.

#### C.14.7 Where to go

- Follow evacuation directions; assemble where told.
- A reception centre will be set up for uninjured survivors and relatives.

#### C.14.8 Checklist

- [ ] Exits noted on every journey
- [ ] Safety briefing listened to
- [ ] Phone charged
- [ ] Someone knows my travel plans

### C.15 Mass evacuation and displacement

#### C.15.1 Risk in the UK

Mass evacuation can follow flooding, wildfire, a chemical or radiation release, or a terrorist attack.
The NRR's coastal flooding scenario assumes "a large number of people would require evacuation and
shelter, with a significant proportion of these requiring assistance".
<sup id="ref-C-2"><a href="#note-C-2">[2]</a></sup>

#### C.15.2 Official plan

- **Evacuation and Shelter Guidance** (Cabinet Office, 2014) is the planning framework: risk-based
  planning, evacuation, shelter, short-term shelter and **rest centre** management.
  <sup id="ref-C-36"><a href="#note-C-36">[36]</a></sup>
- Local authorities open **rest centres** and arrange emergency accommodation; the police lead
  evacuation; the voluntary sector staffs rest centres.

#### C.15.3 Warning

- Emergency Alert, police loudhailer, door-to-door knocking, BBC local radio.
- Flood warnings for flood-related evacuation (see C.1.3).

#### C.15.4 Before

- Keep a grab bag (see F.6) with documents, medication, cash and a charger.
- Agree a meeting point and an out-of-area contact.
- Know your nearest rest centre and two routes there.
- Arrange transport if you have mobility needs; register with the PSR.
- Plan for pets — many rest centres cannot accept animals.

#### C.15.5 During

1. Leave when told; do not delay.
2. Turn off gas, electricity and water if safe.
3. Take your grab bag, medication, documents, phone and charger.
4. Lock up and leave a note saying where you have gone.
5. Follow the designated route.
6. Register at the rest centre — this is how relatives find you.
7. Help neighbours who need assistance.

#### C.15.6 After

- Do not return until the area is declared safe.
- Register with the council for help with housing, insurance and support.
- Tell relatives you are safe; use official missing-person channels rather than social media rumours.
- Seek mental health support if needed (see F.8).

#### C.15.7 Where to go

- Rest centres announced by your council and the police.
- Community hubs for food, clothing, shelter and advice.
- Family or friends outside the affected area.

#### C.15.8 Checklist

- [ ] Grab bag ready
- [ ] Meeting point and out-of-area contact agreed
- [ ] Nearest rest centre known
- [ ] Pet arrangements made
- [ ] Documents and medication ready

### C.16 Reservoir and dam failure

#### C.16.1 Risk in the UK

**Reservoir/dam collapse** is a named risk in the National Risk Register.
<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup> Large reservoirs above towns are regulated under
the Reservoirs Act 1975, with statutory inspection by qualified civil engineers. [VERIFY: current Act
and regulator]

#### C.16.2 Official plan

- Reservoir owners must maintain and inspect their structures and prepare on-site plans.
- Local authorities include reservoir failure in their off-site planning; the Environment Agency is
  the enforcement authority in England. [VERIFY]

#### C.16.3 Warning

- Emergency Alert, police loudhailer, door-to-door.
- Early signs: unusual changes in reservoir level, seepage or cracking at the dam, sudden muddy water
  downstream.

#### C.16.4 Before

- Check whether you live in a downstream flood-risk area from a reservoir — the Environment Agency's
  long-term flood risk service includes reservoirs where data is available.
  <sup id="ref-C-3"><a href="#note-C-3">[3]</a></sup>
- Know your escape route to higher ground.

#### C.16.5 During

1. Move to higher ground immediately — do not wait for confirmation.
2. Do not drive or walk through the flood path.
3. Follow emergency services' directions.

#### C.16.6 After

- Stay away from the dam and the flood path until declared safe.
- Avoid contaminated water and debris.
- Document damage for insurance.

#### C.16.7 Where to go

- Higher ground, away from the valley floor.
- Rest centres announced by the council.

#### C.16.8 Checklist

- [ ] Reservoir flood risk checked for my address
- [ ] Route to higher ground known
- [ ] Grab bag ready

### C.17 Space weather and electromagnetic events

#### C.17.1 Risk in the UK

**Severe space weather** is a named risk in the National Risk Register.
<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup> A major geomagnetic storm can induce currents in
power grids, disrupt satellite navigation and high-frequency radio, and damage satellites. There is
no UK-specific plan for a man-made electromagnetic pulse (EMP) published for the public. [VERIFY]

#### C.17.2 Official plan

- The **Met Office** runs the UK's **Space Weather Operations Centre**, which issues forecasts and
  alerts to government, industry and infrastructure operators. [VERIFY: current service name]
- Grid operators take mitigating action (reducing transformer load, avoiding certain switching
  operations).

#### C.17.3 Warning

- Met Office space weather alerts; aurora visible unusually far south.
- For a man-made EMP there would be no warning — equipment would simply fail.

#### C.17.4 Before

- Keep a battery radio, torches, cash and paper documents.
- Keep electronic equipment you rely on unplugged when not in use.
- Have a plan for medical equipment that depends on mains power.

#### C.17.5 During

- Assume power, telecoms and satellite navigation may fail.
- Listen to the radio.
- Turn off and unplug sensitive equipment to reduce surge risk.

#### C.17.6 After

- Have electrical equipment checked before reuse.
- Expect GPS-based services (navigation, some payment systems, timing) to be unreliable for a period.

#### C.17.7 Where to go

- Stay at home unless told otherwise.

#### C.17.8 Checklist

- [ ] Battery radio and torches
- [ ] Cash
- [ ] Medical equipment plan
- [ ] Paper maps

### C.18 Drought, heat and water shortage

#### C.18.1 Risk in the UK

**Drought** and **high temperatures and heatwaves** are separate named risks in the National Risk
Register.<sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup>

#### C.18.2 Official plan

- The **Environment Agency** (England) and **Natural Resources Wales** manage water resources and can
  impose **hosepipe bans** and, in severe drought, drought orders and emergency drought orders.
- Water companies publish drought plans; the government convenes the National Drought Group.
- UKHSA issues heat-health alerts with the Met Office. [VERIFY: current alert naming]

#### C.18.3 Warning

- Hosepipe bans and drought permits/orders announced by water companies and regulators.
- Heat-health alerts and Met Office warnings.

#### C.18.4 Before

- Store water (3 litres/person/day for 3 days).
- Fix leaks and fit water-saving devices.
- Know your water company's restrictions and priority codes.

#### C.18.5 During

- Follow hosepipe bans and restrictions.
- In heat: hydrate, avoid the hottest hours, keep rooms cool, check on vulnerable people.
- Never leave children or animals in parked cars.

#### C.18.6 After

- Check for heat damage to pipes and foundations after a drought.
- Continue water-saving measures as advised.

#### C.18.7 Where to go

- Cool public spaces announced by your council during heatwaves.

#### C.18.8 Checklist

- [ ] Water stored
- [ ] Heat plan for vulnerable household members

## D. LOCATIONS AND INFRASTRUCTURE

### D.1 Shelters and safe places

**There is no public civil-defence shelter network in the UK.** UK doctrine is to **shelter in place**
or to evacuate to **rest centres** and temporary accommodation arranged by responders.
<sup id="ref-D-1"><a href="#note-D-1">[1]</a></sup>

| Type of place | Good for | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Internal room, cellar or basement | Explosion, blast, fallout | Go low, away from windows, protect head and chest |
| Underground car park under your home | Blast and fallout | Away from the entrance, away from glass |
| Any sturdy building other than the one involved | Chemical release, smoke | Close external doors and windows; shut off fans and air conditioning; close vents including trickle vents |
| Upper floor of a sturdy building | Flooding | Take water, food, radio, phone, medication |
| Large open area with little vegetation | Wildfire | Go low, cover skin and airways |
| Rest centre (council-run) | Evacuation | Register on arrival so relatives can find you |

**Why not a car or a tent:** official advice is explicit that **vehicles and tents give inadequate
protection** from a chemical release.<sup id="ref-D-1"><a href="#note-D-1">[1]</a></sup>

### D.2 Rest centres and community hubs

- **Rest centres** are opened by local authorities during evacuation. They provide shelter, warmth,
  food, information and registration.
- **Community hubs** offer "food, clothing, shelter and advice during a flood".
  <sup id="ref-D-2"><a href="#note-D-2">[2]</a></sup>
- After a chemical or radiation incident you may be **checked and decontaminated before going to a
  rest centre**.<sup id="ref-D-1"><a href="#note-D-1">[1]</a></sup>
- **Note on pets:** many rest centres cannot accept animals. Plan an alternative now.

### D.3 Hospitals and emergency departments

- **England:** NHS England, with integrated care boards; **27 designated Major Trauma Centres** in a
  tiered system of Major Trauma Centre → Trauma Unit → Local Emergency Hospital across 22 major trauma
  networks.<sup id="ref-D-3"><a href="#note-D-3">[3]</a></sup><sup id="ref-D-4"><a href="#note-D-4">[4]</a></sup>
- **London** has four Major Trauma Centres: King's College Hospital, The Royal London Hospital,
  St George's Hospital and St Mary's Hospital.<sup id="ref-D-5"><a href="#note-D-5">[5]</a></sup>
- **Scotland:** 14 territorial NHS Boards plus 7 national bodies, including the Scottish Ambulance
  Service and Public Health Scotland.<sup id="ref-D-6"><a href="#note-D-6">[6]</a></sup>
- **Wales:** NHS Wales comprises Local Health Boards, trusts and three special health authorities.
  <sup id="ref-D-7"><a href="#note-D-7">[7]</a></sup>
- **Northern Ireland:** six Health and Social Care Trusts, including the Northern Ireland Ambulance
  Service.<sup id="ref-D-8"><a href="#note-D-8">[8]</a></sup>
- **Air ambulance:** a charity-funded network represented by Air Ambulances UK; you reach it through
  **999**.
- **Emergency department pressure (England, September 2026):** record **2.44 million** A&E
  attendances, **806,707** ambulance incidents, **94.9%** adult acute bed occupancy, **13,647**
  patients a day medically fit for discharge, and a waiting list of **7.4 million** (August 2026).
  <sup id="ref-D-9"><a href="#note-D-9">[9]</a></sup>

**Practical consequence:** in a major incident, expect long waits. Hospitals triage by clinical need,
not by arrival order. Do not go to an emergency department unless you need emergency care.

### D.4 GP out-of-hours, pharmacies and repeat prescriptions

- **111** is the front door for urgent non-emergency care: out-of-hours GP, dental, mental health
  triage and emergency prescriptions. **111 online** works from age 5; call for under-fives. Text
  relay: **18001 111**.<sup id="ref-D-10"><a href="#note-D-10">[10]</a></sup>
- **Repeat prescriptions:** request them up to **5 working days** before you need them.
  <sup id="ref-D-2"><a href="#note-D-2">[2]</a></sup>
- **Community pharmacies** can advise on minor illness and provide some medicines without an
  appointment.
- **Stockpiles in the supply chain:** hospital medicines suppliers must hold at least **8 weeks'**
  stock; NHS Supply Chain holds about **4 weeks**.
  <sup id="ref-D-11"><a href="#note-D-11">[11]</a></sup>

### D.5 Police, fire and ambulance stations

- **Police:** 43 territorial forces in England and Wales, Police Scotland, the PSNI, plus national
  agencies. Find your local station through **police.uk**; non-emergency **101**.
- **Fire and rescue:** separate services per nation. [VERIFY counts]
- **Ambulance:** regional NHS ambulance trusts in England; national services in Scotland, Wales and
  Northern Ireland.
- **Write down now:** your nearest police station, fire station, A&E, urgent treatment centre, GP
  surgery, pharmacy and vet — plus two routes to each.

### D.6 Government and crisis centres

| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| **COBR** (Cabinet Office Briefing Rooms) | Cross-government crisis decision-making |
| **Civil Contingencies Secretariat** (Cabinet Office) | Resilience policy, National Risk Register |
| **UK Health Security Agency** | Health protection in England |
| **Environment Agency** | Flood warnings and defences in England; incident hotline **0800 80 70 60** |
| **Met Office** | Weather warnings; CHEMET; space weather |
| **Office for Nuclear Regulation** | Nuclear safety and security |
| **Health and Safety Executive** | COMAH and pipelines |
| **National Cyber Security Centre** | Cyber incidents |
| **Local Resilience Forum** | Multi-agency planning for your area |
| **Local authority** | Rest centres, emergency accommodation, public health |

### D.7 Water: reservoirs, treatment works, flood defences

- **Water companies** supply drinking water by region; **Scottish Water** and **NI Water** in the
  devolved nations.
- **Reservoirs** above towns are regulated and inspected; reservoir failure is a named national risk.
  <sup id="ref-C-1"><a href="#note-C-1">[1]</a></sup>
- **Flood defences:** the Environment Agency on main rivers and the coast; lead local flood
  authorities on surface water and ordinary watercourses; internal drainage boards in low-lying land.
- **Reservoir flood risk** is included in the Environment Agency's long-term flood risk service where
  data is available.<sup id="ref-C-3"><a href="#note-C-3">[3]</a></sup>

### D.8 Energy

- **National Energy System Operator (NESO)** balances electricity nationally.
- **Distribution network operators** run the local grids; gas is distributed by regional gas networks.
- **Rota disconnection** in a national shortfall: your area is identified by a **load block letter**
  and each rotation lasts about **3 hours**.
  <sup id="ref-C-11"><a href="#note-C-11">[11]</a></sup>
- **Priority Services Register** for advance warning and extra support.
  <sup id="ref-C-12"><a href="#note-C-12">[12]</a></sup>
- **Nuclear power stations** (ONR-regulated sites) include Hinkley Point B and C, Sizewell B,
  Hartlepool, Heysham 1 and 2, Torness and Dungeness B [VERIFY operating status].
  <sup id="ref-C-32"><a href="#note-C-32">[32]</a></sup>

### D.9 Telecommunications

- Four mobile network operators plus virtual operators; **Emergency Alerts** run on **4G and 5G**.
  <sup id="ref-A-4"><a href="#note-A-4">[4]</a></sup>
- **105** for power cuts; **111** for NHS; **999/112** for emergencies.
- In a telecoms failure, **emergency calls roam onto any available network**.
  <sup id="ref-C-11"><a href="#note-C-11">[11]</a></sup>

### D.10 Transport

- **Strategic Road Network** in England: National Highways; **Transport Scotland**, **Traffic Wales**
  and **DfI Roads** in Northern Ireland.
- **Rail:** Network Rail owns the infrastructure; Great British Railways is the planned guiding mind
  [VERIFY current status]. Accidents are investigated by the **RAIB**.
- **Air and sea:** AAIB and MAIB investigate; air ambulance and HM Coastguard respond.
- **Flooding closes roads and railways** — never assume a route is open; check before travelling.

### D.11 High-risk sites

- **COMAH sites:** upper-tier and lower-tier; searchable public register.
  <sup id="ref-C-25"><a href="#note-C-25">[25]</a></sup>
- **Major Accident Hazard pipelines** regulated by HSE.
  <sup id="ref-C-28"><a href="#note-C-28">[28]</a></sup>
- **Nuclear sites:** see C.10.1 for the full ONR list.
  <sup id="ref-C-32"><a href="#note-C-32">[32]</a></sup>
- **Military sites:** not published; keep clear of exercises and convoys.

### D.12 Maps

- **Flood risk:** check your long-term flood risk (rivers, sea, surface water, reservoirs,
  groundwater) and the 5-day forecast.<sup id="ref-C-3"><a href="#note-C-3">[3]</a></sup>
- **Emergency Alerts history:** GOV.UK/alerts lists current and past alerts.
  <sup id="ref-A-4"><a href="#note-A-4">[4]</a></sup>
- **COMAH register:** searchable by area.
  <sup id="ref-C-25"><a href="#note-C-25">[25]</a></sup>
- **Community Risk Register:** your Local Resilience Forum's published risk register.
  <sup id="ref-B-12"><a href="#note-B-12">[12]</a></sup>
- Keep **paper maps** of your area; mobile navigation fails with the network.

## E. NATIONAL RESERVES AND CONTINUITY

### E.1 Oil, gas and fuel reserves

- The UK holds **strategic fuel obligations** as an IEA and EU member; stocks are held commercially
  and by government. [VERIFY current obligation and volume]
- **Gas storage capacity in the UK is small relative to demand**, which makes the country sensitive
  to supply shocks; disruption to Russian gas supplies was removed as an NRR risk in 2026 because UK
  reliance fell.<sup id="ref-B-11"><a href="#note-B-11">[11]</a></sup>
- **Fuel rationing** would require emergency regulations under Part 2 of the Civil Contingencies Act,
  within the limits set out in B.2.

### E.2 Food and water

- The UK does **not** hold a national food stockpile for the public. Official advice is that
  households keep **3 days** of non-perishable food and water.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>
- **Water** is held by water companies as treated water in the network and in service reservoirs;
  mains water typically holds **2 to 3 days'** treated water.
  <sup id="ref-D-1"><a href="#note-D-1">[1]</a></sup>
- If supply fails, water companies distribute bottled water and tankers; your council announces the
  distribution points.

### E.3 Medical stockpiles

- **UK-wide CBRN stockpiles** and **UK-wide pandemic stockpiles** (antibiotics, antivirals, vaccines,
  fluids, needles/syringes), plus **England-only pandemic PPE**.
  <sup id="ref-D-11"><a href="#note-D-11">[11]</a></sup>
- **Hospital medicines:** suppliers must hold at least **8 weeks'** stock; NHS Supply Chain holds
  about **4 weeks**.<sup id="ref-D-11"><a href="#note-D-11">[11]</a></sup>
- **Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland** hold their own PPE stocks.
  <sup id="ref-D-11"><a href="#note-D-11">[11]</a></sup>
- **Stable iodine** is distributed around nuclear sites with a credible radioactive iodine risk.
  <sup id="ref-C-34"><a href="#note-C-34">[34]</a></sup>

### E.4 Rationing and priority

- **Electricity:** in a national shortfall, **rota disconnection** by load block, roughly 3 hours at a
  time.<sup id="ref-C-11"><a href="#note-C-11">[11]</a></sup>
- **Priority Services Register** customers are flagged to suppliers for extra support.
  <sup id="ref-C-12"><a href="#note-C-12">[12]</a></sup>
- **Healthcare and emergency services** are prioritised in any allocation scheme. [VERIFY]

### E.5 Money and payments

- **Keep cash** — a mix of coins and notes — because card machines and ATMs need power and network.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>
- Emergency calls roam onto any available network, so a phone may still work for 999 even when your
  own operator is down. <sup id="ref-C-11"><a href="#note-C-11">[11]</a></sup>
- Banks must maintain operational resilience; outages are reported to regulators. [VERIFY]

### E.6 Continuity of government and essential services

- **COBR** and the **Civil Contingencies Secretariat** coordinate national response; **Lead
  Government Departments** own each risk.
- **Local Resilience Forums** coordinate local response; **rest centres** and emergency accommodation
  are the local authority's job.
- **Military Aid to the Civil Authorities (MACA)** provides Defence support when civil capacity is
  exceeded.
- **Emergency regulations** under Part 2 of the Civil Contingencies Act are time-limited to **30
  days** and cannot amend the Human Rights Act.
  <sup id="ref-B-5"><a href="#note-B-5">[5]</a></sup>
- **Nuclear command:** Standing Defence Nuclear Command ensures lawful authority survives; letters of
  last resort are held by submarine commanders. [VERIFY]

## F. YOUR PREPAREDNESS

### F.1 Official advice: three days

The UK government's advice, relaunched as the **Be Ready** campaign on **22 October 2026**, is to be
ready for **three days** of disruption. The three-year campaign is explicitly aligned with France,
Ireland and the Netherlands, and more than **500 organisations** had signed up at launch.
<sup id="ref-F-1"><a href="#note-F-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Why three days:** in the first days of an emergency, services are disrupted, responders are
stretched, and help cannot reach everyone at once. Being ready for three days means the emergency
services can prioritise those who need them most.
<sup id="ref-F-1"><a href="#note-F-1">[1]</a></sup>

**Build it gradually.** The official advice is to check what you already have, note what is missing,
buy a little at a time during normal shopping, check every six months, and talk to your neighbours.
<sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>

### F.2 Emergency kit (official list)

| Category | Items |
|---|---|
| **Water** | Bottled water for 3 days — **3 litres per person per day** for drinking; more for cooking and washing |
| **Food** | **3 days** of non-perishable food needing no refrigeration or cooking: tinned meat, fish or vegetables, dried fruit, nuts. Buy food you would normally eat. Tin opener |
| **Hygiene** | Hand sanitiser, wet wipes, bathroom products |
| **Light and warmth** | Torch and spare batteries (safer than candles); blankets, sleeping bags, warm clothing |
| **Communication** | Battery or wind-up **FM radio**, with frequencies written down; charged **power bank** for phones and compatible medical devices |
| **Money and documents** | Cash (coins and notes) **and** cards; printed copies of passports, birth certificates, insurance details and medical equipment instructions; written contact list, medication list and doses |
| **Medical** | First aid kit (plasters, bandages, thermometer, antiseptic, eyewash, sterile dressings, gloves, tape, tweezers); essential medication — request repeats up to **5 working days** ahead |
| **Baby** | Ready-to-feed formula (you may not be able to boil water), nappies, wipes |
| **Pets** | Pet food and bottled water |

<sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>

**Also useful:** a spare bag ("grab bag") ready to take if you must leave quickly; a winter car kit
(torch, in-car charger, warm clothes and blankets, high-visibility clothing, jump leads, food and
drink, shovel, first aid kit).<sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>

### F.3 Water and food quantities

| Item | Per person per day | 3 days | 7 days |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water (drinking only) | 3 litres | 9 litres | 21 litres |
| Water (drinking, cooking, hygiene) | 4–5 litres | 12–15 litres | 28–35 litres |
| Food energy | 2,000–2,500 kcal | ~7,000 kcal | ~16,000 kcal |

Official guidance gives 3 litres per person per day for drinking, noting that **more is needed for
cooking and washing**.<sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>

### F.4 Medication and medical needs

- Keep a written list of all medicines, doses and allergies.
- Request repeat prescriptions up to **5 working days** before you need them.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>
- If you rely on powered medical equipment, ask your supplier and care provider for a plan now.
- Register for the **Priority Services Register** — it exists exactly for this.
  <sup id="ref-C-12"><a href="#note-C-12">[12]</a></sup>
- Telecare and landline providers may supply a battery backup for a minimum of **one hour** — ask
  yours.<sup id="ref-C-11"><a href="#note-C-11">[11]</a></sup>

### F.5 Power, light, heat and cooking

| Need | Without power | Safety |
|---|---|---|
| Light | Torch, head torch, battery lamp | **Torches, not candles** |
| Phone | Power bank, car charger | Keep one device charged for alerts |
| Heat | Blankets, sleeping bags, warm clothing; heat one room | Never use a barbecue, camping stove or generator indoors (carbon monoxide) |
| Cooking | Camping stove or barbecue | **Outdoors only** |
| Water | Stored water, bottled water | Never drink from streams or wells |
| Food | Non-perishable food needing no cooking | Discard food that has warmed up |

Before power returns: **switch off and unplug** irons, ovens, heaters, TVs and computers; keep fridge
and freezer doors closed.<sup id="ref-C-11"><a href="#note-C-11">[11]</a></sup>

### F.6 Communication plan

1. Agree a **meeting point** near home and one outside your area.
2. Agree an **out-of-area contact** — someone who can relay messages.
3. Write phone numbers **on paper** and keep them with your kit.
4. Agree **who checks on whom**, especially anyone living alone.
5. Add emergency contacts and medical information to your phone ("ICE" / "Medical ID").
6. Swap details with neighbours; consider a street messaging group.
7. Give your **noodle plan** to family, neighbours and the out-of-area contact.
8. Teach children when and how to call 999.
<sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>

### F.7 Documents and money

- Printed copies of passports, birth certificates, insurance policies, medical equipment
  instructions and your medication list.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>
- Keep them with your kit, in a waterproof bag.
- Keep cash — coins and notes — as well as cards.
- Keep a written record of insurance policy numbers and emergency contacts.

### F.8 Mental health and resilience

- **Samaritans: 116 123** — free, 24/7, any phone. Welsh-language line: **0808 164 0123**.
  <sup id="ref-F-2"><a href="#note-F-2">[2]</a></sup>
- **NHS 111**, then select the **mental health option**; 111 online also triages mental health.
  Call **999** or go to A&E if life is at risk.
  <sup id="ref-F-3"><a href="#note-F-3">[3]</a></sup>
- **Childline: 0800 1111** for under-19s — the number will not appear on the phone bill.
  <sup id="ref-F-3"><a href="#note-F-3">[3]</a></sup>
- **Cruse Bereavement Support: 0808 808 1677**, with 80+ local branches across England, Wales and
  Northern Ireland.<sup id="ref-F-4"><a href="#note-F-4">[4]</a></sup>
- **Northern Ireland: Lifeline 0808 808 8000** — free, 24/7 crisis response.
  <sup id="ref-F-5"><a href="#note-F-5">[5]</a></sup>
- Practical steps: keep routines, sleep, eat, move, limit news to set times, and help others — helping
  is one of the strongest protections for your own mental health.
  <sup id="ref-F-1"><a href="#note-F-1">[1]</a></sup>

### F.9 Vulnerable people

- **Older people and anyone with a health condition:** check on them in heat, cold, and outages.
- **Children under 5** qualify a household for the Priority Services Register.
  <sup id="ref-C-12"><a href="#note-C-12">[12]</a></sup>
- **People with disabilities:** plan evacuation routes that work without lifts; the PSR covers reduced
  mobility.
- **People dependent on electricity for medical equipment:** agree a plan with your supplier and care
  provider, and register for the PSR.
- **People who do not speak or read English well, or are not online:** the Be Ready campaign was built
  with charities representing under-represented groups.
  <sup id="ref-F-1"><a href="#note-F-1">[1]</a></sup>
- **People in insecure housing or using food banks** are explicitly considered by the campaign.
  <sup id="ref-F-1"><a href="#note-F-1">[1]</a></sup>
- **Pets:** plan now — many rest centres cannot take animals.

### F.10 Community and volunteering

- Talk to your neighbours; swap contact details and agree who checks on whom.
  <sup id="ref-C-6"><a href="#note-C-6">[6]</a></sup>
- Join or start a street or building messaging group.
- Volunteer: the **British Red Cross** (including its UK resilience work), **St John Ambulance**,
  **RNLI**, mountain rescue, and local flood action groups and community flood wardens.
  <sup id="ref-C-5"><a href="#note-C-5">[5]</a></sup>
- "Play your part" is part of the official GOV.UK/Prepare guidance.
  <sup id="ref-F-6"><a href="#note-F-6">[6]</a></sup>

### F.11 Preparedness for businesses and organisations

- Businesses are **Category 2 responders** if they operate essential services, and must cooperate
  with responders.
- Every organisation should have a **business continuity plan** and a **flood plan** if relevant.
  <sup id="ref-C-5"><a href="#note-C-5">[5]</a></sup>
- The NRR is written for risk and resilience practitioners, including businesses and voluntary
  organisations.<sup id="ref-B-6"><a href="#note-B-6">[6]</a></sup>

## G. LAW, RIGHTS AND INSURANCE

### G.1 Emergency legislation

| Instrument | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Civil Contingencies Act 2004, Part 1** | Duties on Category 1 and 2 responders: risk assessment, planning, business continuity, warning and informing the public, information sharing |
| **Civil Contingencies Act 2004, Part 2** | Emergency regulations, subject to the triple lock, proportionality, 30-day lapse and the limits in B.2 |
| **Emergency Powers Act 1920** | The predecessor regime; repealed and replaced by the 2004 Act |
| **Coronavirus Act 2020** | Gave sweeping temporary powers during the pandemic; most powers have expired or been suspended. [VERIFY current status] |
| **Public Order Act 1986 / 2023** | Public order offences, protests and processions |
| **Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (PACE)** | Police powers of stop, search, arrest and detention |
| **Terrorism Act 2000** | Terrorism offences, proscription, stop and search powers |
| **Counter-Terrorism and Border Security Act 2019** | Further counter-terrorism powers, including at borders |
| **Reserve Forces Act 1996** | Call-out of reservists |
| **Radiation (Emergency Preparedness and Public Information) Regulations 2019** | Nuclear emergency planning and public information |
| **COMAH Regulations 2015** | Major accident hazard sites |
| **Pipelines Safety Regulations 1996** | Major accident hazard pipelines |

<sup id="ref-G-1"><a href="#note-G-1">[1]</a></sup>

### G.2 Your obligations

- **Follow the instructions of the emergency services.** In an evacuation, delay puts lives at risk.
  <sup id="ref-C-7"><a href="#note-C-7">[7]</a></sup>
- **Do not obstruct** responders; do not go to look at an incident.
- **Follow restrictions** imposed under emergency regulations, which can cover movement, gatherings
  and the requisition of property.
- **Reservists** can be called out under the Reserve Forces Act 1996 for national danger and warlike
  operations.
- **There is no conscription** in the UK today. [VERIFY]
- Emergency regulations **cannot** require military service or ban industrial action.
  <sup id="ref-B-4"><a href="#note-B-4">[4]</a></sup>

### G.3 Your rights

- **Emergency regulations cannot amend the Human Rights Act 1998**, and must be proportionate and
  limited to what the emergency requires.
  <sup id="ref-B-4"><a href="#note-B-4">[4]</a></sup>
- Regulations **lapse after 30 days**, and Parliament scrutinises them.
  <sup id="ref-B-5"><a href="#note-B-5">[5]</a></sup>
- Offences created under emergency regulations are limited to summary offences with a maximum of
  **3 months' imprisonment** or a level 5 fine.
  <sup id="ref-B-4"><a href="#note-B-4">[4]</a></sup>
- **You have the right to information about risk.** Category 1 responders must warn and inform the
  public, and REPPIR requires prior information to the public near nuclear sites.
  <sup id="ref-B-1"><a href="#note-B-1">[1]</a></sup><sup id="ref-C-33"><a href="#note-C-33">[33]</a></sup>
- **Data protection:** your personal data is protected by the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act
  2018; Emergency Alerts do not collect your phone number or location.
  <sup id="ref-A-4"><a href="#note-A-4">[4]</a></sup>

### G.4 Insurance: what is covered

**Flood Re** is the key scheme for flood cover. It is a reinsurance scheme, not an insurer:

- It runs until **2039**.
- Every UK home insurer pays a **Levy** raising **£160 million a year**.
- Insurers cede flood risk to Flood Re at a **fixed premium based on your council tax band**.
- There is a **standard excess of £250** per policy.
- Claims are paid by your insurer and then reimbursed by Flood Re.
- **Eligibility:** individual policyholders; owner or immediate family lives there (or it is
  unoccupied); domestic **Council Tax band A–H**; private residential; a single unit or a building of
  **no more than 3 units**; individually insured; **built before 1 January 2009**; in England, Wales,
  Scotland or Northern Ireland. It excludes blocks of more than three flats, housing association
  buildings and business-rated premises.
- **Build Back Better:** up to **£10,000** for flood-resistance and resilience measures on top of
  repairs, if your insurer offers it.
<sup id="ref-G-2"><a href="#note-G-2">[2]</a></sup>

| Event | Home and contents | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Flood** | Usually covered, and Flood Re supports high-risk homes | Check your policy; Flood Re eligibility rules above |
| **Storm** | Normally covered | Check the policy wording and excess |
| **Terrorism** | Household policies normally cover terrorism | **Pool Re** covers **commercial** property, backed by an unlimited government guarantee |
| **War** | Normally **excluded** | War exclusions are standard. [VERIFY] |
| **Nuclear** | Normally **excluded** | Standard nuclear exclusion. [VERIFY] |
| **Pandemic** | Business interruption generally not covered | Some policies offer extensions |

**Pool Re** was established in 1993 after reinsurers withdrew from terrorism cover. It is a mutual
reinsurer providing members with an **unlimited government guarantee** to insure commercial property
against terrorist acts. A 2022 strategic review cut overall pricing by **20%** and extended the
guarantee by a further **five years**.
<sup id="ref-G-3"><a href="#note-G-3">[3]</a></sup>

### G.5 Government compensation

- **Bellwin scheme** (England): discretionary emergency financial assistance to local authorities,
  under s.155 of the Local Government and Housing Act 1989. It reimburses eligible spending above a
  threshold set at **0.2%** of the authority's calculated annual budget. Eligible spending includes
  safeguarding life and property, rest centres, evacuation and bed-and-breakfast costs, temporary
  mortuaries and emergency food supplies. **Insurable costs are excluded.**
  <sup id="ref-G-4"><a href="#note-G-4">[4]</a></sup>
- **Flood Recovery Framework** (England): activated at ministerial discretion for wide-area severe
  weather. Core schemes include a **Community Recovery Grant** (around **£500** per household), a
  **Business Recovery Grant** (around **£2,500** per SME), **100% council tax discount** for at least
  three months, and **100% business rates relief** for at least three months for properties with a
  rateable value under **£10 million**.
  <sup id="ref-G-5"><a href="#note-G-5">[5]</a></sup>
- **Storm Henk (January 2024) payments** illustrate the scale: up to **£500** for flooded
  households, up to **£2,500** for SMEs, up to **£5,000** from the Property Flood Resilience Repair
  Grant, up to **£25,000** from the Farming Recovery Fund, plus council tax and business rates relief.
  <sup id="ref-G-6"><a href="#note-G-6">[6]</a></sup>
- **Always claim on insurance first.** Government schemes generally exclude damage that is
  insurable.

### G.6 Documenting damage

1. **Photograph everything before you move or clean anything** — every room, every item.
2. Keep receipts for emergency repairs, accommodation and replacement items.
3. Keep a written timeline: when the incident happened, when you evacuated, who you spoke to.
4. Report to your insurer as soon as you can; keep the claim reference.
5. Keep damaged items for inspection unless they are a health hazard.
6. Register with your council — this is how you access recovery grants.
